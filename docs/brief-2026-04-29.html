EU AI Act Digital Omnibus — Political Trilogue Agreement Targeted for April 28 (Outcome Pending Confirmation)

AI/ML SaaS/B2B Health Tech
iKey Finding
The second and final political trilogue meeting on the Digital Omnibus on AI was scheduled for April 28, 2026 in Strasbourg, with a political agreement anticipated. Both the European Parliament (569–45 vote, March 26) and the Council have converged on fixed postponement dates: December 2, 2027 for stand-alone high-risk AI systems (Annex III) and August 2, 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products (Annex I). However, as of the time of this brief, no official press release confirming a deal has been located — this must be verified within hours. If the Omnibus is not agreed before August 2, 2026, the original high-risk obligations apply as written with no extension.
§Deep Analysis
The Digital Omnibus on AI (Commission proposal: November 19, 2025) is the most consequential piece of EU AI legislation in motion. The core effect, if passed in time, is to defer the most operationally burdensome AI Act obligations — Annex III conformity assessments, quality management systems, EU AI database registration, and human oversight mechanisms — by approximately 16 months (August 2026 → December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems). Parliament and Council are aligned on: (a) fixed hard dates rather than standards-conditioned dates (Commission's original proposal); (b) a new Article 5 prohibition on AI systems generating non-consensual intimate imagery ('nudifiers'); (c) a 'strict necessity' standard for processing special categories of personal data for bias detection; and (d) simplified but retained registration obligations for self-assessed non-high-risk systems. Key open issues as of the April 28 session include: exact scope of sectoral AI safety integration (Parliament proposes moving Annex I-A product categories into sectoral laws — medtech clients affected), watermarking/synthetic content deadline (Parliament: November 2, 2026; Council: February 2, 2027), and residual GPAI model supervision questions. Critical planning implication: even if the Omnibus passes, Article 50 transparency obligations (chatbot disclosure, AI-generated content labelling) and GPAI model obligations remain active. Clients who have been treating August 2026 as a 'soft' deadline because of the Omnibus must understand that several obligations are NOT moved, and the postponement only benefits those with formally documented compliance preparation underway. The EDPB and EDPS have formally criticised certain simplifications in Joint Opinion 1/2026 (April 24, 2026), signalling that privacy regulators will apply maximum scrutiny to post-Omnibus compliance claims. The legal uncertainty between now and Official Journal publication means the safest compliance posture is to continue against the original August 2 timeline.

EDPB Publishes First Harmonised EU DPIA Template — Public Consultation Open Until June 9, 2026

SaaS/B2B AI/ML Health Tech E-commerce
iKey Finding
On April 14, 2026, the EDPB published the first-ever harmonised Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) template (Version 1.0, adopted March 10, 2026), accompanied by an explanatory 'Explainer' document. The template is currently voluntary, but following the June 9 consultation close, all EU and EEA national DPAs will be required to adopt it as their sole standard or as a meta-template to which national templates must align. The template explicitly addresses the intersection with the EU AI Act, including the Article 26(9) obligation on AI system deployers to use provider-supplied information when fulfilling their DPIA obligations under GDPR Article 35.
§Deep Analysis
This is the most operationally significant GDPR compliance tool published since the regulation entered into force in 2018. The practical effect for clients operating across multiple EU jurisdictions is significant: until now, DPIAs have been structured differently in France (CNIL's open-source PIA tool), Germany (Standard Data Protection Model), Spain (AEPD guide), and others — creating compliance fragmentation and cross-border audit uncertainty. Once finalised, the EDPB template establishes a minimum documentation standard that all DPAs across the EEA will accept. Key structural features of the template: seven sections (Section 0: processing overview; Section 1: systematic description; Section 2: technical and organisational measures; Section 3: necessity and proportionality; Section 4: risk assessment and action plan; Section 5: consultation; Section 6: DPO opinion and controller sign-off). The template makes a methodological distinction between 'design risk' (inherent in the processing's architecture) and 'incident risk' (operational and security threats) — a conceptual refinement relevant for any client deploying AI or SaaS systems with complex data flows. AI Act integration: Section accommodates the Article 26(9) AI Act obligation, meaning any client deploying a third-party AI system will need to document the provider's Article 13 transparency information within the DPIA. This creates a direct procurement and contract management implication — clients must ensure their AI vendor agreements require delivery of Article 13-compliant documentation. For Health Tech clients processing special category data on a large scale, this template will become the expected standard for any DPA audit or prior consultation request. The consultation is an opportunity to submit feedback that shapes the final template — practically valuable for clients whose processing activities are not well-served by the current draft (e.g., complex research platforms, multi-party clinical trial architectures).

EDPB Guidelines 1/2026 on Scientific Research Data Processing — Major Clarifications for Health Tech and AI/ML Clients; Consultation Closes June 25

Health Tech AI/ML SaaS/B2B
iKey Finding
On April 16, 2026, the EDPB adopted Guidelines 1/2026 on the processing of personal data for scientific research purposes — the most significant GDPR research guidance since 2019. The Guidelines clarify when 'broad consent' is permissible (must cover a defined research domain, not generic future purposes), set out six indicative factors for what qualifies as 'scientific research' under GDPR, address the compatibility presumption under Article 5(1)(b), and establish that private entities may conduct qualifying scientific research. The public consultation runs until June 25, 2026. These guidelines are directly relevant to Health Tech clients running real-world evidence studies, AI/ML clients training models on health or research datasets, and SaaS clients providing platforms to research organisations.
§Deep Analysis
This is a pivotal document for Health Tech clients. The GDPR has always provided research-specific derogations (Articles 5(1)(b), 9(2)(j), 89), but their practical application has been deeply uncertain — generating compliance paralysis in clinical data reuse, biobank operations, and AI model training on medical data. The Guidelines address several longstanding ambiguities: (1) Scope of 'scientific research': The EDPB introduces six key indicative factors — methodical and systematic approach, adherence to recognised ethical standards, verifiability and transparency, autonomy and independence, objectives contributing to general scientific knowledge, and societal well-being — even where commercial interests are also pursued. Critically, private entities and profit-oriented research can qualify, but the label 'research' is not self-certifying. Activities like 'platform optimisation,' 'proprietary model development,' and 'real-world evidence generation for commercial purposes' face harder scrutiny — these are areas where AI/ML clients training on health data must carefully map their activities against the six factors. (2) Broad consent vs. standard consent: Broad consent remains permissible where research purposes are not fully known at data collection, but the Guidelines tighten this significantly — the consent must cover a defined research area (e.g., 'oncology research'), not generic 'research purposes.' Where future activities fall outside original consent scope, 'dynamic consent' for specific projects is required. This will require review of existing consent forms for Health Tech clients. (3) Compatibility presumption: Where data collected for non-scientific purposes is reused for research, the Article 5(1)(b) compatibility presumption applies. However, data collected for scientific research and reused for non-scientific purposes (e.g., commercial product development) requires a full compatibility assessment — a direct risk for AI companies training models on research datasets. (4) Joint controllership: The EDPB emphasises that active participation in defining a research protocol indicates controller status. Clinical trial sponsors remain controllers (or joint controllers) even if processing only pseudonymised data. This has substantial implications for SaaS platforms providing research infrastructure — they may be joint controllers rather than mere processors. (5) Individual rights limitations: The Guidelines clarify when the right to erasure and right to object can be legitimately restricted — erasure requests can be rejected when erasure would 'seriously impair' research objectives, but this must be documented. These are currently in draft/consultation status and may be modified before finalisation.

🟠 High High Confidence #03 of 03

Garante and CNIL Issue Coordinated Guidelines on Email Tracking Pixels — Compliance Windows Now Running

E-commerce SaaS/B2B Health Tech
iKey Finding
On April 17, 2026, the Italian Garante adopted Guidelines on the use of tracking pixels in email communications (pending Official Gazette publication). France's CNIL had previously issued a Recommendation on the same topic on March 12, 2026. Both authorities treat tracking pixels in email as terminal access tools subject to the ePrivacy Directive, requiring prior consent. The CNIL compliance window is three months from March 12 (deadline: approximately June 12, 2026). The Garante grants six months from Official Gazette publication. A key Garante innovation is the right to granular consent withdrawal — users must be able to opt out of pixel tracking specifically while continuing to receive emails.
§Deep Analysis
This coordinated enforcement initiative directly affects any client using email marketing platforms that include open/click tracking — which encompasses virtually all SaaS/B2B, E-commerce, and Health Tech clients operating in Italy or France. The near-simultaneous publication by two of the EU's most active DPAs signals a coordinated enforcement trend, likely linked to the EDPB's 2026 CEF action on transparency obligations. The legal basis: both authorities characterise email tracking pixels as tools that access information stored on the recipient's device (i.e., the act of the email client rendering the pixel causes a network request), bringing them within the scope of Article 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive as transposed in France (Article L.34-5 CPCE) and Italy. This analysis is parallel to the treatment of website cookies. The Garante's most significant innovation is granular consent withdrawal: data subjects must be able to opt out of pixel tracking while continuing to receive email communications. This requires a two-layer mechanism in every email footer — a link for unsubscribing from the mailing list AND a separate mechanism for deactivating tracking only. This is more demanding than the CNIL's approach, which requires an accessible withdrawal link but does not require the same level of granularity between list subscription and tracking consent. Practical implications: (1) Email marketing platforms must be configured to honour per-user tracking opt-outs without triggering unsubscription. (2) For emails already in the client's database (CNIL): within approximately six weeks, send a clear information notice to all existing recipients enabling them to object to future tracking. (3) Privacy notices and cookie/tracking policies must be updated. (4) For SaaS clients that provide email functionality as part of their product (e.g., marketing automation, CRM with email), this creates a product compliance obligation, not just a marketing operations issue — their customers may be subject to these rules. (5) The CNIL deadline of approximately June 12 is operationally tight. Health Tech clients sending health-related email communications face heightened risk given the sensitivity classification of health data. Note: The Garante Guidelines are pending Official Gazette publication — the six-month compliance window has not yet started running. Monitor the Official Gazette for the publication date.

Key Themes

Cross-sector patterns · 29 April 2026
01

EU Is Standardising Compliance Infrastructure While Simultaneously Testing Enforcement Readiness

Three of this brief's five items reflect a single meta-theme: the EU is moving from an era of principle-setting into an era of compliance standardisation and enforcement operationalisation. The harmonised DPIA template ends eight years of format fragmentation. The scientific research guidelines close a decade-long interpretive gap. The Digital Omnibus rationalises the AI Act's implementation architecture. At the same time, the CNIL's recruitment enforcement sweep and the Garante/CNIL pixel coordinated action signal that DPAs are not waiting for perfect frameworks — they are enforcing now, with existing tools, in specific high-risk sectors. Clients who have invested in compliance infrastructure are entering a period where that investment pays off in audit resilience. Clients who have deferred are running out of runway.
02

Email Tracking and Consent Infrastructure: The Next GDPR Enforcement Battleground

The near-simultaneous adoption of email tracking pixel guidelines by both the Garante (Italy) and CNIL (France) — both characterising pixels as ePrivacy-regulated terminal access tools — reflects a convergent regulatory position that is likely to spread across additional DPAs. This mirrors the trajectory of cookie consent enforcement, which began with France and Italy and eventually became an EU-wide enforcement priority. SaaS clients whose products include email tracking functionality, and E-commerce clients relying on email open/click data for marketing automation, face a structural compliance challenge that will only intensify. The Garante's granular consent withdrawal requirement — separate opt-outs for email receipt and tracking — is more demanding than any current commercial ESP product natively supports, creating a near-term product compliance gap.
03

AI Act Compliance: The Gap Between Published Obligations and Enforcement Infrastructure Is the Real Risk

The Digital Omnibus debate and the uneven state of national market surveillance authority designation reveal a structural tension: the AI Act's obligations are legally clear, but the enforcement machinery is not yet in place in most Member States. This creates a false sense of safety for non-compliant clients. The EDPB's Joint Opinion 1/2026 explicitly criticises certain Omnibus simplifications, signalling that privacy regulators — who ARE operational and active — will scrutinise AI system deployments through the GDPR lens even before AI Act enforcement is fully functional. The practical implication: GDPR-based AI enforcement (via DPIA obligations, automated decision-making rules, data minimisation requirements) is happening now, regardless of where the AI Act implementation stands.
